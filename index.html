01 · The accomplishment

I didn't learn to code. I built a company.

CircleRootTech is a technology company I am building from the ground up on one conviction: software should be deliberate, it should be durable, and it should be made for the people who use it rather than at their expense. That's the whole brief. Not scale, not an exit, not shipping fast enough to flood a store with things nobody asked for.

The products are the output. The company is the thing I actually made: registered in Utah, with an operating handbook, a strategic roadmap, a pricing model, and a standard for what ships — run by one person, alongside a full-time job.

Learning React Native was the easy part. The hard part was deciding what a company is for. There is an enormous amount of software being generated right now by people who can prompt their way to a build, can't get it through review, can't support it, and never asked whether it should exist. I'm not a vibe coder shipping volume. I don't compete on how much I can put out. I compete on whether the thing is worth having.

Money isn't the goal. Revenue keeps the company alive so it can keep improving — that is its entire job. What I'm actually building for is access: that the people a product was made for can afford it, can trust it, and get a better version of it every year they stay.

The charter

  • No ads, no data selling, ever. Not a growth-stage decision to revisit later. It's the reason the company exists.
  • Priced for access, not extraction. If the choice is between a bigger number and more people being able to use the thing, more people wins. Every time.
  • Every product comes from something I've lived. Community, safety, caregiving, connection, getting hired. I don't build for markets I've only read about.
  • A three-year improvement cadence. Apps get better on a schedule, driven by user feedback — not abandoned at v1.0, not churned for the sake of a changelog.
  • Status is honest. Two apps are live. The rest are in development, in review, or being rebuilt — and this page says so instead of rounding up.
  • I test every fix myself before it ships. On my own device, every time.

02 · The standard

A conviction isn't real until it costs you something.

Anyone can write a charter. This is what mine has cost me — every rejection, every rebuild, every time I chose the slower thing on purpose. It's the only part of this page that proves the rest of it.

2024 Baseline LLC formed. First line of React Native. No engineering background — a Master of Education, an English degree, and sixteen years of programs.
Q1 2026 The Shielded Path Shipped Child-safety app, live on the App Store. Firebase anonymous auth for kids' devices, expiring pairing codes, live device heartbeat.
Q2 2026 The Shielded Path Pulled — by me Approved and live, but the Safari filtering users were paying for couldn't work without an entitlement Apple wasn't going to grant. I pulled my own approved app rather than sell a promise I couldn't keep, and rebuilt the architecture on DNS instead. Shipping the wrong thing is not shipping.
Q2 2026 LaunchMaxx 4.3(a) — Spam Rejected for looking generic. Redesigned the app, rebuilt the paywall with live pricing from the store, and routed my API keys through a Cloudflare Worker so no key ever ships in the bundle.
Q2 2026 CloseOnes Rejected ×3 Wrong button IDs on the marketing site. A subscription that had to be submitted on its own review lane, not attached to the version. A one-letter casing bug in the RevenueCat entitlement. Found each one, fixed each one.
Q2 2026 CloseOnes Shipped v1.1.5 approved and live. Magic-link sign-in, yearly auto-renewing subscription, no ads and no data sold.
Q3 2026 Kongvene Shipped Mahjong circle manager, approved and live on the App Store. RSVP seating at tables of four, automatic substitute promotion, organizer/player roles enforced in Firestore security rules — not just hidden in the UI.
Q3 2026 Alongside Coming soon Companion app for the sandwich generation, caring for aging parents and kids at once. Heading into App Store submission.
Q3 2026 ApplyWorthy New The one below. Sixteen years of the job-seeker problem, finally in code.

Every fix in this log was diagnosed and verified by me, on my own device, before it went back to review. That habit turned into a service: diagnosing AI-generated apps that keep failing App Store review, and fixing them for real.

04 · Newest product

ApplyWorthy

Job seekers spend hours on applications that were never going to be read — knocked out by an applicant tracking system before a human sees them. ApplyWorthy checks the friction before you apply, so you spend your hours where they can land.

I built it because I watched it happen for sixteen years, from the side of the desk where you have to tell someone why they never heard back. It ships as a licensed tool for workforce-development organizations — the people already doing this work, given something that scales past one caseworker at a time.

Node · ATS detection engine · profile-matched scoring · B2B2C licensing model

See the full ApplyWorthy page →

1Paste the job posting link before you start the application.
2ApplyWorthy identifies which applicant tracking system is behind it.
3It scores the friction — how many screens, how much re-typing, what quietly disqualifies you.
→You decide where your hours go. Apply, tailor first, or skip it.

05 · Strongest revenue potential

The Grove

HOA communication mostly happens in scattered group texts, buried emails, and paper flyers taped to a mailbox kiosk. The Grove gives a board one place to post announcements, collect dues, and manage maintenance requests — and gives residents one app instead of four.

Residents always use it free. The HOA board pays a small per-unit fee, the same way a company pays for software its employees use — starting at nothing for small communities, scaling with the neighborhood.

React Native · Expo · Firebase · multi-tenant Firestore · join-code onboarding

→One app, not four. Dues, maintenance, safety, and lost pets, all in the same place.

07 · Working with AI

I'm not an AI researcher. I'm someone who ships with it.

Every app in this portfolio was built by one person with AI as a working partner — architecture, code review, debugging, strategy. That's not a novelty anymore. What's still rare is knowing where the partnership ends.

AI will confidently help you build something that cannot work. It helped me build a child-safety app around a capability iOS was never going to grant me — and no amount of prompting would have caught that. A person had to. That experience is most of what I know about working with these tools: they compress the building, and they raise the price of bad judgment. The judgment is still the job.

It also turned into a service. There is a flood of AI-generated software right now that can't pass App Store review, and the people who generated it often can't read it well enough to find out why. I can — because I've been rejected for most of the reasons already.
